# Reader-Facing Draft Skill

Use this skill inside `chapter-materialization` when drafting or expanding chapter Markdown. It turns chapter packages into reader-facing prose on the first drafting pass instead of producing a planning memo that later needs translation into book prose.

Working policy:

- Draft from the reader-style contract, chapter brief, storyline map, and evidence map at the same time. The first visible chapter text should already read like a book chapter for the declared readers.
- Before writing visible prose, record the intended reader entry and chapter movement in the brief or drafting notes: opening form, reader need, main claim, section movement, table/figure role, and closing transition where useful. Scenes, questions, and examples are options selected for this book and chapter, not required plan fields. Use the plan to draft the chapter body; do not expose the plan itself as manuscript prose.
- For every major section, name the reader-facing job before drafting: what problem the reader sees, what explanation changes their understanding, what consequence follows for curriculum/evaluation/project design, and how the section hands off to the next section. This prevents the first draft from becoming a list of framework elements.
- In evidence-dense chapters, build a connected account around the reader's clinical, technical, or historical question. Synthesize sources by the decision or mechanism they illuminate; compare studies only on dimensions their populations, inputs, endpoints, and designs support. Avoid a run of disconnected paper summaries, and make meaningful differences between studies legible to the reader.
- Draft source-supported claims together with their source locators or house-style citations, so later citation work can preserve the claim's scope and support. When the book uses numbered citations, maintain first-appearance order as the prose develops and reconcile it with the final reference list.
- Keep production metadata out of manuscript prose. Chapter task, core question, chapter thesis, target budget, source refs, asset status, QC notes, and blockers belong in chapter briefs, manifests, QC reports, comments, or handoff refs; they must not appear as opening paragraphs or headings in the reader-facing chapter body.
- Treat visible workflow language as a drafting blocker. Manuscript prose must not explain the production process, current version state, source-of-truth mechanics, QC status, blocker status, manifest state, PDF/export readiness, or internal review plan to the reader. Phrases such as `当前版本`, `最终书稿`, `本章讨论`, `下一章将`, `source of truth`, `QC`, `blocker`, `manifest`, `后台`, `公开可观察`, `公开资料显示`, `教育实验观察窗口`, and `观察它如何强调` belong in workflow refs only, unless quoted as source material with explicit editorial purpose.
- Open chapters in the form established by the book's style contract and sample. A chapter may begin with historical development, a study finding, a concept, a practical question, a scene, or another suitable entry. Define terms where the chosen narrative makes their meaning useful to the reader.
- Shape paragraphs so explanation and evidence lead to their consequence or next idea. Scene or question -> explanation -> consequence -> transition is one useful pattern among several; adapt paragraph movement to the subject rather than repeating a fixed sequence.
- Use headings as reader navigation, not planning labels. Headings should name what the reader is about to understand, not the internal job the section performs.
- When examples or cases are part of the chapter, integrate them into its prose or the form specified by the style contract. Tables and figures should support an argument the prose has prepared; case boxes are optional and should follow the book's established design.
- For quantitative research findings, preserve the analysis unit and denominator, study design and cohort split, data-collection timepoint, reference standard, and endpoint that give a result its meaning. Distinguish patients from images/frames, development from validation cohorts, and prediction targets such as pathological response from clinical response; explain differing denominators where a paper reports them.
- Write the first production draft to its chapter budget. A thin chapter that names all sections but lacks developed argument and the evidence, explanation, transitions, or figure/table interpretation required by its chapter contract remains `seed_in_progress`; cases are included when the style and source material call for them.
- Measure the draft with the chapter's declared extent unit and inclusion scope. Apply that same measure during planning and QC, and do not infer manuscript length from page counts that change with Word/PDF layout.
- Treat "reader-facing revision" as an exception or review response, not the normal path. If a chapter needs that pass because the first draft reads like a memo, record it as a drafting-gate failure and update the chapter-production pattern before continuing with more chapters.
- If a required figure is missing, keep a human-readable caption or figure note only when it helps review; do not expose implementation refs such as manifest filenames, asset status files, or prompt paths inside the main reader text.
- Treat reader-facing drafting as a first-draft quality target, not a separate late polish stage. A later reader-facing revision pass may still be used when review finds drift, but the workflow must not rely on that pass to remove predictable production scaffolding.

This skill does not bypass chapter budget gates, source grounding, figure asset blockers, or owner review. It changes the expected shape of the initial chapter prose.
